Item(by='lhorie', descendants=None, kids=[26020533], score=None, time=1612392635, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26019972, text='My understanding of the trade-offs have to do with primarily with devil-in-the-details stuff, i.e. how the feature actually gets implemented.<p>AFAIK, there are two major philosophies: implement boxed types (which Go kinda already has, via `interface {}` being implemented as tuple of (type, value), or very aggressive static analysis (like Rust).<p>The problem with the former is what you get in Java: can&#x27;t make generics of primitives, and runtime perf issues with boxing&#x2F;unboxing when you want to e.g. sort terabytes worth of ints.<p>Conversely, the problem w&#x2F; the Rust approach is that the cost is shifted over to the compiler, which has to figure out how to emit optimal primitive-handling code given that the entry point of a generic call tree might be very far away (or even unknown at compile time). This leads to slow compilation times.<p>For go specifically, I think there&#x27;s some amount of worry wrt overlapping features (given how go does interfaces) and the subsequent loss of clarity, given that go falls closer to the one-way-of-doing-things side of the scale.<p>All of these concerns are of the death-by-thousand-paper-cuts variety: can I live with a bit slower compilation times? Yes. Until it&#x27;s so slow that I can&#x27;t. Will I love generic collections? Yes. Until I&#x27;m neck-deep in a J2EE-like over-architected mess. Etc. Recall that go comes from Google, where minimizing the amount of crap in a billion LOC codebase is considered very important, so concerns like trading off simplicity and compiler speed for other benefits tend to be thought in terms of how the negatives look in the absolute worst case scenario.')
