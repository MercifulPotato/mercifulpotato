Item(by='droptical', descendants=None, kids=[25999626], score=None, time=1612262563, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=25998625, text='I prefer to use the term &quot;tamper-evident&quot; when describing these things - you can totally fiddle with logs, but the property you likely want is that this can be detected.<p>As you say, Merkle trees are fairly straight-forward, but beyond integrity checking a file they have some other nice properties - as the &quot;operator&quot; of a Merkle tree based log, you can efficiently [O(log N)] convince an observer that:\n- you&#x27;ve only grown the log by appending new entries since the last time they looked (&quot;consistency proof&quot;)\n- a given entry they hold is indeed included in the log (&quot;inclusion proof&quot;)<p>if you change an entry in the log (or claim something is in the log when it&#x27;s not) it becomes impossible for you to prove both of those properties (unless you&#x27;re powerful enough to also break what&#x27;s called the &quot;second pre-image resistance&quot; of the cryptographic hash you&#x27;re using).<p>Note that for these two properties, an observer doesn&#x27;t need to download or inspect the full contents of the log, just a handful of hashes in the Merkle tree which they can then use to recreate the &quot;tree head hash&quot; you mentioned. These &quot;tree heads&quot; are the state they store and are just a few hundred bytes.<p>There are generally a few types of log client: \n- one type is putting things into the log\n- one type are wanting to use what&#x27;s stored in the log to make a decision of some kind (&quot;should I trust this site&quot;, &quot;should I install this binary&quot;, &quot;am I seeing the true history of events&quot;, etc.) and they won&#x27;t trust that data unless they are convinced it is in the log\n- another can verify the accuracy&#x2F;validity&#x2F;goodness of things in the log (e.g. in CT it&#x27;s the domain owner who is in the unique position of saying &quot;yes, I asked for this cert to be created&quot;, for binaries it might be &quot;I&#x27;m the authorised creator of these binaries and, yup, that was me&quot; or &quot;I&#x27;m a respectable malware scanning company, and this binary doesn&#x27;t match any known signatures&quot;, &quot;I&#x27;m an auditor and these transactions are complete and correct&quot;, etc. - you can probably think of many more examples!)<p>In this light, you can think of a tamper-evident log as being a sort of &quot;reliable transport&quot; to connect these different types of clients together, and importantly, where they all can prove to each other (using those light weight tree heads + consistency&#x2F;inclusion proofs) that they&#x27;re seeing the same set of data in the log - or, if they can&#x27;t prove it - they have the cryptographic evidence of operator of the log operator having done something <i>unexpected</i>.<p>If you have a spare few mins, have a look at the Claimant Model docs here: <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;github.com&#x2F;google&#x2F;trillian&#x2F;tree&#x2F;master&#x2F;docs&#x2F;claimantmodel" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;github.com&#x2F;google&#x2F;trillian&#x2F;tree&#x2F;master&#x2F;docs&#x2F;claimant...</a>\nwhich talks in a bit more detail about this &quot;transport&quot; idea and the concept of discoverability which it brings.<p>Hope that&#x27;s helpful&#x2F;interesting!<p><i>[Disclaimer: I work on CT, Trillian, and a bunch of other related projects.]</i>')
