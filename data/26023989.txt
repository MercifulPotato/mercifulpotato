Author of the piece here<p>Thanks for the feedback, this prompted me to add an afterword to the piece about the dangers of optimising too hard.<p>To me there&#x27;s a pretty important difference between &#x27;it is systematically bad to do anything about inefficiency&#x27; and &#x27;sometimes inefficiency is protecting something important&#x27;. I strongly agree with the second one, but strongly disagree with the first one. I think that, empirically, there often are free wins, such as making learning more efficient. And sometimes inefficiency and balance is important, but sometimes it&#x27;s not, and the skill is telling the difference.<p>For example, I think leisure time is great, but this is because it&#x27;s time to relax, take breaks and do what I enjoy. Not just because it&#x27;s time that I spend not being productive. If I notice a bunch of time in my schedule not being productive, but also not enjoying myself, eg commuting to work, finding ways to swap that for more true leisure time is a good way to reduce wasted motion<p>You might enjoy a post I wrote on the importance of leaving Slack and spare capacity in your life: <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.neelnanda.io&#x2F;blog&#x2F;38-slack" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.neelnanda.io&#x2F;blog&#x2F;38-slack</a> - essentially on the importance of leaving some inefficiency, so you can be flexible and reactive, and seize new opportunities.')
