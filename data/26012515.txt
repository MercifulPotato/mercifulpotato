Item(by='raattgift', descendants=None, kids=None, score=None, time=1612349652, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=25998634, text='It wasn&#x27;t theology at all.  It&#x27;s just that he was equipped with a less rigorous analogue of the Raychaudhuri equation and an intuition about the focusing theorems one could extract from it.   In short: a universe with reasonable distributions of matter in it (galaxies) would collapse to a point eventually.   Since the Earth was billions of years old (with improved estimates growing in age), the universe must be older still, yet it had not collapsed.   Either there was a lot less matter in the universe than there appeared (and again, better observations kept finding more &quot;nebulae&quot; and then galaxies) or something acted against the tendency to collapse.   A simple scalar multiplier on the metric tensor was a straightforward and simple way to proxy for the latter.  &quot;Static&quot; in this context simply meant &quot;non-collapsing over billions of years&quot;, with the value depending on the averaged matter-density.<p>Later he and de Sitter discovered that the multiplier could be set to zero (and consequently omitted from the field equations) while allowing an arbitrarily long -- eternal, even -- expansion of a hot big bang universe, even for arbitrarily large average matter-densities.<p>It wasn&#x27;t until decades after his death that better observations demanded the return of the small positive cosmological constant.   This time it wasn&#x27;t to prevent total collapse, but rather to account for the gravitational drag of early galaxy clusters on each other not slowing the Einstein-de Sitter expansion (and even later speeding the expansion up).<p>The cosmological constant term was always a free parameter.   It&#x27;s just that there was no point in writing it down if it was measured as zero.')
