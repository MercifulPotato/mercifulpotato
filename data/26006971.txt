Item(by='jabl', descendants=None, kids=[26007193], score=None, time=1612301252, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26005871, text='My understanding is that the typical way to do parallel decode of a variable length ISA is to speculatively decode from every valid offset, then throw away those which turned out to be wrong.<p>So x86 implementations start to decode at [n, n+1, n+2, n+3, etc.] bytes. Then if turns out that, say, the instruction at offset n was 2 bytes, throw away whatever work was done for decoding at n+1 and continue decoding the n+2 etc.<p>For RISC-V with the C (compressed) extensions, instruction boundaries are 16-bit aligned, so you can do a similar strategy like above for x86 except you speculatively decode at offsets [n, n+2, n+4, etc.]. See e.g. the SonicBOOM paper.<p>Of course, speculatively decoding instructions only to throw them away costs power. I have no hard numbers, but I do wonder why RVC didn&#x27;t specify that an &quot;instruction bundle&quot; is 32-bit aligned and consists of either a 32-bit instruction or a pair of 16-bit instructions? That would have made decoding simpler, though at a cost of worse density.<p>The problem with this approach is that you get more and more such wasted decoding effort when you try to make a wider decoder. Then again, uop caches exist for a reason and generally work well, they are an excellent choice if you have a hard to decode ISA.')
