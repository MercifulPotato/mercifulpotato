Item(by='pdonis', descendants=None, kids=[26021536], score=None, time=1612397783, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26018094, text='<i>&gt; It is not free will in the sense that you can spontaneously decide it for yourself.</i><p>If &quot;spontaneously&quot; means &quot;outside of the laws of physics&quot;, then of course I agree. I just don&#x27;t think that kind of &quot;free will&quot; is the only possible kind of free will, nor do I think that impossible kind of free will is the kind of free will that matters.<p><i>&gt; I can choose to eat vanilla yogurt or plain yogurt. That is the narrative my brain hallucinates. The reality behind the illusion is that the choice was made before I even am aware of the &quot;story I tell myself&quot; that I chose one or the other.</i><p>Of course this is true in the sense that, as far as we can tell, the brain process of you being <i>aware</i> of your choice happens either at the same time as, or after, the brain process that makes your choice. But so what? All those processes are still happening in <i>your</i> brain.<p>Compare what you just described with this scenario: you want vanilla yogurt, but I force you to have plain yogurt instead because I believe it&#x27;s healthier for you. Here there are still processes happening in your brain, which, if I weren&#x27;t there, might well have led to you eating vanilla yogurt--but because I <i>am</i> there and I force you to eat plain yogurt, <i>your</i> brain processes do not determine what kind of yogurt you eat--instead, <i>my</i> brain processes do.<p>Do you see any meaningful difference between those scenarios? I sure do. (And note that I think that difference is meaningful even if it is <i>true</i> that the plain yogurt would be healthier.) If you don&#x27;t, then we have a fundamental disagreement that I don&#x27;t think any discussion can resolve.')
