The justification for it was never X11 doesn&#x27;t work.  It was always &quot;it&#x27;s becoming impossible to work on&quot;.<p>As it happens, Wayland does solve a problem I have.  The HDMI port doesn&#x27;t work under X11 on my Lenovo X1 2nd gen laptop.  It does under Wayland.  Granted, that&#x27;s because no one wants to work on X11 any more, so new or unusual stuff doesn&#x27;t get added.  (The X1 gen2 is indeed unusual: the HDMI port comes off the Nvidia card rather than the intergrated graphics.  The X1 gen3 fixes that nit.)<p>Which brings us the full circle: Wayland may fix no issues he has now, but the X11 code base being so much of a pig will mean he has issues with it in the future.<p>I&#x27;ve now moved to Wayland, and it was under sufferance.  It&#x27;s forced me to use Gnome3.  The Gnome3 taskbar and I are not compatible (&quot;what a complete waste of space&quot; springs to mind), so I&#x27;m not that happy about it.  Debian&#x27;s alternatives system for selecting a Window Manager (now the compositor under Wayland) seems to be completely broken.<p>But Wayland itself: a few paper cuts, touchpads not working after suspend and things like that - nothing serious.  But my god it&#x27;s fast, and amazingly remote X11 connections (ie, &quot;ssh -X&quot;) still just work.  And the code base is much smaller.<p>People are comparing Wayland to systemd, to me it&#x27;s not at all like systemd.  Systemd was more complex, in lines of code literally 100&#x27;s of times larger than then things it replaced, and never delivered on it&#x27;s speed promises, and seeks to replace everything with Systemd.  On all those metrics, Wayland is the reverse - it even encourages mutiple implementations.')
