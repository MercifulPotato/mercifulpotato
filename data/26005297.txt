Item(by='rspeele', descendants=None, kids=[26005650, 26006016], score=None, time=1612293578, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26005040, text='That&#x27;s finance!<p>What&#x27;s really fun is that Alice and Charlie may not even know that they&#x27;ve lent their stock. Their broker, having a substantial amount of the stock pooled across all accounts they manage, may lend it out and still let Alice and Charlie trade their &quot;share&quot; -- since if Alice wants to sell, the broker can always sell on of the other shares from their pool. As long as the broker keeps a decent amount of shares still in their possession, and as long as all accountholders don&#x27;t try to liquidate at the same time, it&#x27;s fine. Even if you DID try to liquidate your position and whoopsie, the broker actually lent out all their shares and there are none left for them to sell, the broker could buy your share obligation with cash and you wouldn&#x27;t know the difference between that and selling it to another shareholder.<p>This is the type of thing that gets some people&#x27;s back up. It all sounds very &quot;precarious&quot; as you say. There is a reaction that says, whoa, that&#x27;s way too complicated and abstracted, this whole thing is a house of cards just waiting to collapse. But it&#x27;s kind of like a long-lived piece of software, which accumulates complexity from the need to deal with a complex world. Only new programmers have the urge to tear it all down and start from scratch -- because old programmers have found that the &quot;new&quot; system ends up reinventing the complexity of the old as it discovers the same problems and missing features of the &quot;simple&quot; approach. Today you see this happening with cryptocurrency.')
