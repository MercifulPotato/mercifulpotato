Let&#x27;s say you flip a coin, have it land on the back of one of your hands, and quickly cover it with your other hand so you can&#x27;t see how it landed.  Despite that, it is no longer spinning: its state is now completely determined.  So what is the probability P that the coin came up heads?<p>In one way to look at it, P=50%, assuming it was a fair coin that generated a uniformly distributed binary variable.  In another way, P is either 100% (the coin in fact came up heads) or else it is 0% (coin came up tails), and &quot;50%&quot; is meaningless: it&#x27;s not Schroedinger&#x27;s coin.  So in this view, people who say 50% are committing the error of (insert long essay here full of fancy words like &quot;epistomology&quot;).<p>Philosophers write books about this issue, plato.stanford.edu has some good articles about probability interpretation that also discuss the topic, and the linked article is more of the same.<p>For the rest of us, there are some basic confusions that beginners and non-mathematically-informed people make, but if you take a few classes or work through a basic textbook, you can understand how significance testing works, what confidence intervals actually measure, etc, and then you can think pretty clearly.  The Bayesian vs frequentist thing is a yawner: if you put one of each in a room, give them the same data and ask them to crunch the numbers, they might disagree on some philosophical abstractions but they&#x27;ll get the same answers.<p>I find Andrew Gelman&#x27;s blog far more interesting than the linked one, at least based on the linked article.  Either way, it&#x27;s definitely worth studying some basic probability theory (understanding the math rather than trying to interpret the topic by navel-gazing) if you&#x27;re a programmer or do anything else in science or technology.')
