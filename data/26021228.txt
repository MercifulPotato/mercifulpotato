Item(by='mdip', descendants=None, kids=None, score=None, time=1612399648, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26019021, text='You bring up some good points and I&#x27;m familiar with the Stratasys products (though it&#x27;s been a few years).<p>You can only slightly &quot;buy your way out of the speed problem&quot;, which is as much dependent on the material as it is the capabilities of the machine.  An 18 hour print for a small item is not unusual at all, for me, but I&#x27;m on a pretty simple printer.<p>A stratasys-style machine wouldn&#x27;t make sense for this application.  I think &quot;high-end desktop quality&quot; FDM would suffice for accessories&#x2F;GameStop (with, perhaps, an option to get items printed in aluminum&#x2F;other exotic materials shipped -- partner with someone), so the aim would be to find a reliable, enclosed (with appropriate filtration[0]), FDM (or similar price&#x2F;quality), pick up 3-5 of them per store -- different sizes -- and spend a mint building software for your customers to use to interface with the printers[1] and automate the hell out of the ugly parts of the printing process (print-bed surface prep&#x2F;part removal, maintenance and cleaning).  And make absolutely sure the model has <i>every</i> convenience feature (like bed leveling, temperature controlled build chamber)<p>I&#x27;d probably limit material types to one (production) and one (support, when necessary).  Optimize all of the hardware for the printers to the specific characteristics of that material and choose a material that prints easily&#x2F;quickly -- PLA is a reasonable choice, prints pretty quickly but is brittle&#x2F;fails over time and has a low melting point, ABS has several toxicity concerns and is finicky as hell to print with, but it&#x27;s cheap and when it prints &quot;right&quot;, it&#x27;s durable.<p>It sounds <i>crazy</i> to do something like this with desktop 3D printers, but it makes the most sense.  $1000 will get you one hell of a box with the ability to print in multiple colors and with more than one hot-end.  It&#x27;s probably, as you mention, not quite there yet -- I know from personal experience, messing around with the slicer software can take some time.  There&#x27;d be a lot of software to be written and probably some additional hardware to design to fit the purposes of the stores printing needs. But, I guess you&#x27;d give the staff something to do while waiting for customers to not come in and buy games. &#x2F;s :)<p>While they&#x27;re at it, throw in a large CNC&#x2F;laser cutter, re-tool to sell dev boards&#x2F;robotics equipment, replace &quot;Game&quot; with &quot;Hacker&quot; and open up several hundred hacker-spaces around the country.  Perhaps that would have worked better for Radio Shack.  But I&#x27;m only half-joking -- every time I walk into small-time game&#x2F;hobby&#x2F;computer shops I find that the (often young) staff there are makers and would fit right in with a little training.<p>[0] This, too, depends on the material.  And you might need to call in the lawyers&#x2F;etc to figure out how things are going to have to be designed to avoid a future class-action from your employees&#x2F;customers due to exposure to fumes.  ABS is known to be problematic; it&#x27;s also cheap and has many other desirable properties.<p>[1] The good news is that there is a <i>ton</i> of open-source pieces to start with.')
