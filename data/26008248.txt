Item(by='brundolf', descendants=None, kids=[26008441], score=None, time=1612306855, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26008120, text='Your link appears to be broken<p>We can debate the value of one-word (or even acronym) vs five-word variable names, but the key factor in my view is that the name holds some sort of intrinsic meaning to the person looking at it, vs being an arbitrary symbol. For example, x and y - despite being one-letter variable names - are perfectly sensible in a context where you&#x27;re talking about euclidean geometry and referring to values on the x and y axes. But they are not reasonable names in the context of business logic where they represent customers or transactions. Alternately, a software shop may establish a convention that generics A and B mean something specific across all contexts in their codebase. A company-specific convention this arbitrary will still increase the barrier for newcomers to comprehend the code, but once learned they will at least be able to re-use the knowledge and A and B will become meaningful to them. In this way, &quot;meaningful&quot; may be relative. Usually meaningful names will take the form of one or more words, but not always; I wouldn&#x27;t say the actual length itself is what&#x27;s important.<p>With that established: by choosing names that have intrinsic meaning to the reader, the reader&#x27;s mind is allowed to skip a step of indirection where they would otherwise have to perform a mental &quot;dictionary lookup&quot; from symbols to concepts. To be frank: study or no study, it&#x27;s literally unbelievable to me that removing this step wouldn&#x27;t help the slightest bit with both comprehension and recall.<p>Addendum: There&#x27;s a further dimension to this which is that code is more than what it does. Take the following function:<p><pre><code>  function m(a, b) {\n    if (a &lt; b) {\n      return a;\n    } else {\n      return b;\n    }\n  }\n</code></pre>\nIs this code wrong?<p>Because the function lacks a meaningful name (or documentation), it&#x27;s not simply hard to tell, it&#x27;s a question with an <i>undefined answer</i> because the intent is not represented in any way. This function currently returns the minimum of the two arguments, but there&#x27;s no indication whether it was meant to return the minimum, or the maximum, or even the sum for that matter.<p>Maybe you could argue that we should be writing comments anyway, but that still moves the meaning across a step of indirection, where you have to go find it instead of seeing it inline. We absolutely should be writing comments, but they don&#x27;t completely replace good naming.')
