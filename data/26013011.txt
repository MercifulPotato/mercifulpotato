vs. &quot;feet&quot;, e.g. 中 (third last in the list) pronounced <i>zhōng</i> as a noun (center, middle) but <i>zhòng</i> as a verb (as in 中的 <i>zhòng dì</i> hit the target).<p>In a reply to a reply, you give the example of English &quot;a&quot; turning into &quot;an&quot; if the following word starts with a vowel. That&#x27;s analogous to 一 (<i>yī</i> one) changing tone depending on the following syllable, e.g. 一個 (<i>yí gè</i> one), 一起 (<i>yì qǐ</i> together).<p>Then there&#x27;s register differences like 血 (<i>xiě</i>&#x2F;<i>xuè</i>&#x2F;<i>xuě</i> informal&#x2F;formal&#x2F;confused for blood) as in 出血 (<i>chū xiě</i> bleed) vs 血壓 (<i>xuè yā</i> blood pressure)<p>And that&#x27;s just the standard language. In everyday speech, people have another set of colloquial vocabulary to draw on, which they may or may not be able to associate with corresponding characters.<p>It&#x27;s not as complex as Japanese, but the relationship between writing and pronunciation isn&#x27;t quite one-to-one for Chinese either.')
