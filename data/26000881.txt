Item(by='WillFlux', descendants=None, kids=None, score=None, time=1612273683, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26000145, text='PLLs are common to many electronics system, if you’re looking for a primer, try <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.analog.com&#x2F;en&#x2F;analog-dialogue&#x2F;articles&#x2F;phase-locked-loop-pll-fundamentals.html" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.analog.com&#x2F;en&#x2F;analog-dialogue&#x2F;articles&#x2F;phase-loc...</a><p>However, from the point of view of these designs, the PLL is an internal black box that allows us to generate an (almost) arbitrary clock frequency.<p>We choose a frequency to meet I&#x2F;O or performance requirements. In this case a pixel clock of ~25 MHz: we have to meet this to generate a valid 640x480 video signal.<p>None of the Exploring FPGA Graphics designs uses a CPU: the logic is all in hardware, often a finite state machine. The trick is ensuring the hardware logic completes within one clock cycle. For 25MHz, each clock cycle is 40ns. If a design is too complex to meet timing, we can break it into multiple steps, similar to pipelining on a CPU[1].<p>You can also run different parts of a design at different clock frequencies, but this introduces the challenge of clock domain crossing[2].<p>For a low-end FPGA, like an Artix-7, you can expect to run a reasonably complex design at 100-200 MHz.<p>[1] <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Classic_RISC_pipeline" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Classic_RISC_pipeline</a><p>[2] <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Clock_domain_crossing" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Clock_domain_crossing</a>')
