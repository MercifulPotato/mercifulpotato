Item(by='motohagiography', descendants=None, kids=[26001240], score=None, time=1612275650, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=25999363, text='Interesting to read a summary of what are essentially Socratic rhetorical techniques. To me the question is, why bother, and what is the conclusion of choosing an atheistic belief? I was a fan of Hitchens, and I took his atheism the same way I take an actor&#x27;s belief in astrology, in that it is a part of who they are, but not what I find interesting about them.<p>There is a western tradition wherein atheists are considered untrustworthy men, as no matter how different or foreign someone is to you, if you can agree there exists a god (by whatever name) who rewards good and punishes evil, and that some intentions of this being have been revealed, this belief is a foundation for a basic level of trust that transcends language.<p>The whole atheist enterprise is premised on an &quot;anti-&quot; identity, where the &quot;-ist&quot; person evaluates their own identity against a perceived reflection of this divine straw-man. A recent pope described &quot;hell&quot; as being &quot;the absence of God,&quot; I suspect atheist hell is the absence of someone to hector with tedious hypothetical arguments for attention. The anti- identity is what we used to call being a punk, or someone who identifies as exempt from the scale of &quot;good,&quot; because they perceive themselves as having been victimized by the bearers of that standard, and they look for others to seduce with a misery-loves-company sympathy. Whether or not one agrees with the morality of that, it&#x27;s a summary of the very roots of what we call western civilization.')
