See [1]. But maybe you&#x27;re thinking of building capacity in a different way.<p>&gt; We refused to risk any individual life in order to potentially save hundreds of thousands or more.<p>This is such an important point. In particular, human challenge trials, in which healthy, low-risk volunteers are infected with a low dose of the virus, could have saved tens of thousands of lives -- at a minimum -- in the U.S. alone. The case for HCTs, including the ethical case, is taken up in [2].<p>In a comment below, someone likens HCTs to the infamous Tuskegee syphilis experiment. The comparison isn&#x27;t fair. In the Tuskegee experiment, people were infected with syphilis were promised medical care that they were later denied. When they were diagnosed with syphilis, they weren&#x27;t even told of the diagnosis. Those conditions are a world apart from the HCTs that Eyal et al. propose in [2]. \n- - - - -<p>[1] <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.bloomberg.com&#x2F;news&#x2F;features&#x2F;2020-10-29&#x2F;inside-operation-warp-speed-s-18-billion-sprint-for-a-vaccine" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.bloomberg.com&#x2F;news&#x2F;features&#x2F;2020-10-29&#x2F;inside-op...</a><p>[2] <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;academic.oup.com&#x2F;jid&#x2F;article&#x2F;221&#x2F;11&#x2F;1752&#x2F;5814216" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;academic.oup.com&#x2F;jid&#x2F;article&#x2F;221&#x2F;11&#x2F;1752&#x2F;5814216</a>')
