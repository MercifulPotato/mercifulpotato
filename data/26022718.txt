Item(by='wahern', descendants=None, kids=[26023249, 26022799], score=None, time=1612414165, title=None, item_type='comment', url=None, parent=26015392, text='&gt; we refused to risk any individual life in order to potentially save hundreds of thousands or more.<p>The concern wasn&#x27;t Trolley problem[1] paralysis, where authorities are afraid to deliberately shift harm to a minority to protect a majority. The concern was vaccine-associated disease enhancement (VADE), which could possibly cause <i>more</i> overall harm and deaths than simply doing nothing at all. It&#x27;s a significant and legitimate concern because not only has it happened before with released vaccines, it has been a particular problem in SARS vaccine research and trials for 20 years. See &quot;Learning from the past: development of safe and effective COVID-19 vaccines&quot;, <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.nature.com&#x2F;articles&#x2F;s41579-020-00462-y" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;www.nature.com&#x2F;articles&#x2F;s41579-020-00462-y</a><p>We had complete candidate vaccines mere days into the outbreak, but nobody was sure they were safe because they were just the latest iterations in long lines of similar candidate vaccines, all eventually failing. They couldn&#x27;t rush trials too quickly because VADE situations might not become apparent without a large pool tracked over an extended period of time so that you can see what happens with reinfections, etc.<p>We got lucky. It seems to be the case that we had just recently turned the corner in resolving many of these barriers. If were were facing COVID-10 (i.e. a SARS pandemic in 2010), we&#x27;d be screwed because in 2010 we were much further away from figuring out how to avoid VADE-like failures in SARS vaccines.<p>[1] <a href="https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Trolley_problem" rel="nofollow">https:&#x2F;&#x2F;en.wikipedia.org&#x2F;wiki&#x2F;Trolley_problem</a>')
