The circuit has a high impedance at the desired radio signal&#x27;s frequency, but a low impedance at all other frequencies.[65]&quot;<p>[...]<p>&gt;&quot;The earliest crystal receivers<p><i>did not have a tuned circuit at all, and just consisted of a crystal detector connected between the antenna and ground, with an earphone across it.</i><p>[1][69] Since this circuit lacked any frequency-selective elements besides the broad resonance of the antenna, it had little ability to reject unwanted stations, so all stations within a wide band of frequencies were heard in the earphone[51] (in practice the most powerful usually drowns out the others). It was used in the earliest days of radio, when only one or two stations were within a crystal set&#x27;s limited range.&quot;<p>[...]<p>An important principle used in crystal radio design to transfer maximum power to the earphone is<p><i>impedance matching</i><p>.[51][71] The maximum power is transferred from one part of a circuit to another when the impedance of<p><i>one circuit is the complex conjugate of that of the other</i><p>; this implies that the two circuits should have<p><i>equal resistance</i><p>.[1][72][73]<p>Opinion: There&#x27;s a lot of great concepts here in this -- the most simplest of radios!')
